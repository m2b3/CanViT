Deep neural networks as models of vision

  • [Timing: planned 0:50–1:10; rehearsed 0:50 (2026-10-25)]
  • [Yohaï's text, 2026-10-25; bold is his emphasis]
  • Deep artificial neural networks have a long history as models of the ventral visual stream.
  • This was originally explored in the context of supervised learning.
  • In 2014, a landmark study by Yamins and colleagues found that the brain predictivity of convolutional neural networks trained to classify objects tracked their performance on this task.
  • But such networks learn from millions of human-provided labels, whereas humans are able to learn a lot about the world purely from visual experience.
  • More recent work has found that so-called unsupervised, or self-supervised networks, which require no annotations, and thus learn purely from experience as well, can also achieve high neural predictivity.

An active vision model can be considered along three axes: making sense of what it sees at a given moment (instantaneous vision); updating a persistent, evolving understanding of the scene, which may in turn inform further processing (memory); and deciding where and at what zoom level to look next (action selection). The first two axes define an observer's ability to understand, persist, and recall visual inputs, while the last defines its input-selection strategy.   • [AI draft: not the authors' text]
  • So what's actually in an active-vision model? Three things. Instantaneous vision: making sense of what you see right now. Memory: keeping a persistent, evolving understanding of the scene. And action selection: deciding where, and at what zoom, to look next.
  • The first two make up what we call the observer. The third is the policy.
  • Prior work often focused on the policy, on learning where to look.
  • No policy can make up for a poor observer. And a general-purpose observer lets you use any policy, at least any of the ones we tested.

Where people look depends on their goal

  • [Timing: planned 0:45–1:00]
  • [AI draft: not the authors' text]
  • And there's one more thing an active model has to deal with. This is Yarbus, 1967: the same painting, the same person looking at it, three minutes each time; the only thing that changes is the question they're asked.
  • Free looking first, and the eyes go all over the place. Ask for the ages of the people, and it's all on the faces; the clothes, and the eyes go down the bodies; how long the visitor was away, and it's back and forth between his face and the family.
  • Where you look depends on your goal. So a model that's meant to serve any task has to handle any sequence of fixations, not just the ones a particular policy would pick.

  • It needs a memory, to keep what it has seen from one glimpse to the next.
  • It should give dense outputs, like segmentation, which most active models can't.

  • And it needs rich world knowledge, as passive foundation models have and prior active models lack: that's the accuracy gap we saw.
  • That's what we call an Active-Vision Foundation Model. Nothing ticks all of these boxes yet; this is the goal.

The Canvas Vision Transformer architecture

  • [Timing: planned 1:15]
  • [AI draft: not the authors' text]
  • Here is our architecture, from the paper. On the left, the Vision Transformer, the backbone, processing the first glimpse. On the right, a new component: the canvas, a memory laid out over the whole scene.
  • Between the backbone's blocks, the two exchange information: the glimpse reads from the canvas, then the canvas is written from the glimpse, three times each per glimpse.
  • Next glimpse: the glimpse's tokens are discarded, but the canvas stays. So what the model saw before shapes how it processes what it sees now.
  • One more token carries over: the recurrent CLS token, a summary of the scene so far, which we use for classification.
  • And so on, glimpse after glimpse. Transition: for this to work, the backbone and the canvas must agree on where things are.

Scene-Relative Rotary Position Embeddings (SR-RoPE)

Glimpse patchesCanvas patches

  • [Timing: planned 1:00]
  • [AI draft: not the authors' text]
  • The backbone sees the glimpse as an 8 by 8 grid of patches, in the glimpse's own frame. We give each patch its position in the scene, given by the viewpoint.
  • The canvas patches have positions in the same scene coordinates, on a fixed grid. So the backbone, which works in the glimpse's frame, and the canvas, which works in the scene's frame, share one frame of reference.
  • Zoom in: still 8 by 8 patches, now packed into a small part of the scene. The spacing of the positions tells the model the zoom.
  • These positions enter the attention through rotary position embeddings. Transition: now, how the reads and writes work.

Canvas Attention

  • [Timing: planned 1:15]
  • [AI draft: not the authors' text]
  • One read and one write up close: Canvas Attention. It's cross-attention between the glimpse's tokens and the canvas's tokens.
  • The learned projections are all on the glimpse side.
  • The canvas side gets only a normalization and its positions. No projections, no MLPs, no self-attention on the canvas.
  • So the canvas is cheap and can be large, as detailed as dense outputs need. And the payoff: total compute against output resolution. A plain ViT's cost explodes; CanViT's barely moves, even over 21 glimpses.
  • Transition: that's the architecture. Now, how to pretrain it.

  • How do we pretrain this architecture into a foundation model? We want a model that understands the visual world, and that works with any viewing policy.
  • We treat this as representation learning, not as a reinforcement learning problem. Pretraining the model on a learned policy's glimpses would make it adapt to that policy; and if the policy's gradients reach the model, RL's noisy gradients can make training unstable.

Dense latent distillation

  • [AI draft: not the authors' text]
  • We have the architecture. How do we pretrain it into a foundation model? We treat it as representation learning, not as an RL problem. Two things we want.
  • First, the model has to work with any viewing policy. Where people look depends on the goal, as Yarbus showed, and a foundation model serves many goals. Pretrain the observer on one policy's glimpses and it co-adapts with that policy, even with a stop-gradient; with another policy, there's no guarantee. And if the policy is learned with RL jointly with the observer, its gradients make training unstable.
  • Second, it has to understand the visual world. Learning that from scratch takes the kind of compute that went into DINOv3. But DINOv3 already has that knowledge, for a scene seen whole; our model has to learn to build it from glimpses. And no labels anywhere: DINOv3 learned from images alone, and our model learns from DINOv3's features.
  • Our answers: policy-agnostic pretraining, and dense latent distillation.

The canvas

readwrite
  • [AI draft: not the authors' text]
  • So here's the idea. Next to the scene, keep a grid of latent vectors laid over the whole scene, in scene coordinates: the canvas. Before any glimpse, it holds a learned starting state.
  • The first glimpse comes in. It's processed, and it writes into the canvas, at the place in the scene where it was taken. Notice that it doesn't only change its own footprint: it already says something about the rest.
  • The second glimpse, somewhere else. Before writing, it reads the canvas: what the first glimpse left there shapes how this one is processed. Then it writes, in the same scene coordinates, so the two end up in the same place, a single representation of the scene.
  • And so on, glimpse after glimpse. Wherever you look, at whatever zoom, it all accumulates in the same canvas. That's the Canvas Vision Transformer.
