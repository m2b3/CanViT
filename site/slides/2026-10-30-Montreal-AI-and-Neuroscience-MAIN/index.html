Deep neural networks as models of vision

  • [Timing: 0:50]
  • Deep artificial neural networks have a long history
  • as models of the ventral visual stream.
  • Some of the first deep networks to predict its responses well were trained with supervised learning.
  • As shown by Yamins and colleagues in 2014.
  • They found that, among the classification-trained networks, brain predictivity tracked performance on this task.
  • But such networks learn from millions of human-provided labels,
  • whereas humans are able to learn a lot about the world purely from visual experience.
  • [CLICK]
  • More recent work has found
  • that so-called unsupervised, or self-supervised networks,
  • which require no annotations, and thus learn purely from experience as well,
  • can also achieve high neural predictivity.

DINOv3

DINOv3 ViT-B/16, Siméoni et al., TMLR 2026

  • [Timing: 0:45]
  • A notable example of a self-supervised vision foundation model is DINOv3,
  • which came out in summer 2025.
  • Given an image, it produces a feature map,
  • which is a grid of high-dimensional vectors, called patch tokens.
  • To visualize them, as we do here on the right,
  • we can do principal component analysis across the patches in a given image,
  • and map groups of three principal components to RGB channels.
  • [CLICK]
  • These features are spatially organized and also semantically rich:
  • each patch is linearly decodable into human-interpretable categories,
  • as you can see on the right.

DINOv3 and the brain

  • [Timing: 0:35]
  • DINOv3 was at the center of a recent study by Raugel and colleagues
  • that used 7-Tesla fMRI and MEG imaging
  • to disentangle the factors of convergence between brains and the model.
  • [CLICK]
  • A striking finding was
  • that representational alignment between the model and higher brain areas
  • took more training to emerge than for lower areas.
  • [AI draft: not the authors' text] This suggests that for self-supervised foundation models like DINOv3,
  • [AI draft: not the authors' text] scaling up pretraining matters if we aim to capture higher-order neural responses.

What's missing?

  • [Timing: 0:10]
  • These models are great NeuroAI tools,
  • but they're missing something fundamental.
  • Can you guess?
  • [Timing: 0:15]
  • Our vision is inherently active:
  • we constantly shift our gaze
  • to bring our high acuity fovea to regions of interest.
  • [CLICK]
  • In contrast, standard computer vision models
  • process images in a single, uniform-resolution look.

A brief history of deep active computer vision

Mnih et al. 2014, NeurIPS

  • [Timing: 0:35]
  • Drawing inspiration from human vision,
  • so-called deep active vision models,
  • which incorporate virtual eye movements, have been developed.
  • This line of work traces back to Mnih et al's Recurrent Attention Model, in 2014,
  • which was originally applied to very simple tasks such as digit recognition.
  • [CLICK]
  • Since then, there have been a few follow-ups
  • which handle more complex images and tasks.
  • [CLICK]
  • And in recent years, pioneering NeuroAI work has begun leveraging this paradigm.

The wide gap between passive and active computer vision

  • [Timing: 0:45]
  • Although active-vision models are structurally and conceptually compelling,
  • they have struggled to match the visual intelligence of their passive counterparts.
  • This gap is especially striking in so-called dense prediction tasks,
  • such as semantic segmentation.
  • [CLICK]
  • This task involves assigning a class to every location of an image,
  • which requires more spatial understanding than image-level classification.
  • [CLICK]
  • On ADE20K, a standard semantic segmentation benchmark,
  • the few active-vision models that support dense prediction
  • [CLICK]
  • lag dramatically behind the state-of-the-art.

No policy can make up for a poor observer. A general-purpose observer lets you use any policy.

  • [Timing: 0:45]
  • To better understand this gap,
  • it's helpful to consider active-vision models along three axes:
  • instantaneous vision, memory and action selection.
  • The first two axes define what we call the Observer.
  • In other words, the thing that makes sense of visual inputs.
  • The third axis defines the policy, or input selection strategy.
  • [CLICK]
  • The observer and the policy play fundamentally different roles:
  • If the observer is not good, no policy can make up for that.
  • Conversely, if the observer is sufficiently general,
  • it should be compatible with any policy.
  • [CLICK]
  • Most prior active vision works have focused on learning good policies,
  • without much focus on the observer.

Where people look depends on their goal

  • [Timing: 0:35]
  • So, we just talked about a "general-purpose observer".
  • Can we train a general-purpose policy?
  • In a landmark 1967 experimental psychology study by Yarbus,
  • [CLICK]
  • a participant was asked different questions before viewing the same image.
  • The study found that their eye movement patterns, or scanpaths,
  • were strongly dependent on the question they were asked,
  • showing that the "right" viewing policy depends on the task at hand.

The Canvas Vision Transformer architecture

  • [From Yohaï's run-through of 2026-10-27, false starts removed]
  • That motivates why we needed to come up with a new architecture.
  • Our architecture builds upon a standard Vision Transformer for instantaneous processing, but we augment this component with a memory module that we call the canvas.
  • The Vision Transformer is responsible for retinotopic, glimpse-centered visual processing,
  • whereas the canvas starts off without any scene-relevant information at the beginning of an episode, and then gets gradually updated through what we call Canvas Attention read and write operations.
  • [AI draft: not the authors' text] We also carry a recurrent CLS token from one glimpse to the next, a global summary that we read out for classification.
  • Here we see a glimpse coming into the model. As the backbone processes it, it reads from the canvas and writes to it.
  • From that local evidence, the model produces a global understanding of the scene.
  • But of course, it hasn't seen much here: only a cat's ears. It does extrapolate that there might be something like a whole cat in the picture, but it can't know for sure.
  • As it gets more visual evidence, it is able to refine that understanding.
  • [CLICK]
  • Here, with a second glimpse,
  • [CLICK]
  • and now with a third. We can see that the model's understanding becomes progressively sharper.

Scene-Relative Rotary Position Embeddings (SR-RoPE)

Glimpse patchesCanvas patches

  • [From Yohaï's run-through of 2026-10-27, false starts removed]
  • A key component of this process is to handle space correctly.
  • We developed a positional encoding scheme based on a well-established technique called rotary position embeddings, or RoPE,
  • which allows the model to identify where each piece of evidence came from, and relate it to its spatiotopic, scene-wide canvas.
  • Here we can see that the coordinates of each patch in a glimpse are given to the model in scene coordinates; that's why we call this scene-relative.
  • [CLICK]
  • If we take the glimpse elsewhere, those coordinates change,
  • and the model is able to understand the zoom level of that glimpse from the offset between neighboring glimpse patches in canvas coordinates.

Canvas Attention

  • [From Yohaï's run-through of 2026-10-27, false starts removed]
  • To zoom in a bit more on the computational mechanism behind the Canvas Attention reads and writes that I told you about: here's one step of Canvas Attention.
  • You can think of it as a cross-attention mechanism, for those who are familiar with Transformers, which uses the scene-relative rotary position embeddings that I just told you about, with a few twists.
  • [CLICK]
  • One thing that's notable is that the only fully connected linear projections in the architecture lie on the glimpse side.
  • These are somewhat expensive operations, but they're only applied to a relatively low number of glimpse tokens.
  • [CLICK]
  • On the canvas side, we only apply basic normalization operations for stability, and position embeddings where appropriate.
  • There is no self-attention and no multilayer perceptron on the canvas side.
  • [CLICK]
  • This means that the model is able to handle a very large canvas at low computational cost, scaling to very large canvases in a sub-quadratic manner.

Coarse-to-Fine (C2F) and Fine-to-Coarse (F2C)

  • [From Yohaï's run-through of 2026-10-27, false starts removed]
  • To zoom in on how we evaluated the model: we used distinct policies during evaluation, not just the pretraining policies that I told you about before, R-IID and F-IID.
  • The most important is what we call the C2F policy, or coarse-to-fine, which subdivides a scene into quadrants recursively and traverses them sequentially.
  • This policy is deterministic across zoom levels, but it randomizes the visitation order within each zoom level,
  • so it goes from a zoomed-out, low-resolution view of a scene to progressively finer visual evidence.
  • In contrast, F2C, or fine-to-coarse, does the exact opposite: it starts from fine-grained evidence, then gradually zooms out,
  • which is not a very optimal strategy, because to make sense of fine detail, it helps to know the broader context in which this detail is inscribed.

Entropy-Guided Coarse-to-Fine (EG-C2F)

  • [From Yohaï's run-through of 2026-10-27, false starts removed]
  • Another policy that is particularly interesting is what we call the entropy-guided coarse-to-fine policy.
  • It's the same idea as C2F, except that it takes into account the model's own estimate of its uncertainty.
  • For each output location of the model on ADE20K, we can get a probability distribution over classes.
  • [CLICK]
  • Here, for example, the model is quite sure that at this location lies a building, so the entropy is very low.
  • [CLICK]
  • Conversely, after its first glimpse, it's really not quite sure about this cluttered area here: it's split between car, building, road, maybe plant, maybe other things. So the entropy is high.
  • [CLICK]
  • We can turn this into an entropy map,
  • [CLICK]
  • and as we subdivide the scene into quadrants, we can compute the mean entropy over each quadrant.
  • [CLICK]
  • Then we instruct the model to visit the highest-entropy quadrant in priority; here, that's the bottom-right quadrant.
  • Then we eliminate this quadrant from the quadrants it is allowed to visit at this zoom level, and do the same thing again.
