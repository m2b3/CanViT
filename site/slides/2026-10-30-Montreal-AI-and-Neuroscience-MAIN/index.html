Deep neural networks as models of vision

  • [Timing: 0:50]
  • Deep artificial neural networks have a long history
  • as models of the ventral visual stream.
  • This was originally explored in the context of supervised learning.
  • In 2014, a landmark study by Yamins and colleagues found
  • that the brain predictivity of convolutional neural networks trained to classify objects
  • tracked their performance on this task.
  • But such networks learn from millions of human-provided labels,
  • whereas humans are able to learn a lot about the world purely from visual experience.
  • [CLICK]
  • More recent work has found
  • that so-called unsupervised, or self-supervised networks,
  • which require no annotations, and thus learn purely from experience as well,
  • can also achieve high neural predictivity.

DINOv3

DINOv3 ViT-B/16, Siméoni et al., TMLR 2026

  • [Timing: 0:45]
  • A notable example of a self-supervised vision foundation model is DINOv3,
  • which came out in summer 2025.
  • Given an image, it produces a feature map,
  • which is a grid of high-dimensional vectors, called patch tokens.
  • To visualize them, as we do here on the right,
  • we can do principal component analysis across the patches in a given image,
  • and map groups of three principal components to RGB channels.
  • [CLICK]
  • These features are spatially organized and also semantically rich:
  • each patch is linearly decodable into human-interpretable categories,
  • as you can see on the right.

DINOv3 and the brain

  • [Timing: 0:35]
  • DINOv3 was at the center of a recent study by Raugel and colleagues
  • that used 7-Tesla fMRI and MEG imaging
  • to disentangle the factors of convergence between brains and the model.
  • [CLICK]
  • A striking finding was
  • that representational alignment between the model and higher brain areas
  • took more training to emerge than for lower areas.
  • This highlights the importance of scaling up model pretraining
  • if we aim to capture higher-order neural responses.

What's missing?

  • [Timing: 0:10]
  • These models are great NeuroAI tools,
  • but they're missing something fundamental.
  • Can you guess?
  • [Timing: 0:15]
  • Our vision is inherently active:
  • we constantly shift our gaze
  • to bring our high acuity fovea to regions of interest.
  • [CLICK]
  • In contrast, standard computer vision models
  • process images in a single, uniform-resolution look.

A brief history of deep active computer vision

Mnih et al. 2014, NeurIPS

  • [Timing: 0:35]
  • Drawing inspiration from human vision,
  • so-called deep active vision models,
  • which incorporate virtual eye movements, have been developed.
  • This line of work traces back to Mnih et al's Recurrent Attention Model, in 2014,
  • which was originally applied to very simple tasks such as digit recognition.
  • [CLICK]
  • Since then, there have been a few follow-ups
  • which handle more complex images and tasks.
  • [CLICK]
  • And in recent years, pioneering NeuroAI work has begun leveraging this paradigm.

The wide gap between passive and active computer vision

ADE20K mean intersection over union (mIoU, %)

  • [Timing: 0:45]
  • Although active-vision models are structurally and conceptually compelling,
  • they have struggled to match the visual intelligence of their passive counterparts.
  • This gap is especially striking in so-called dense prediction tasks,
  • such as semantic segmentation.
  • [CLICK]
  • This task involves assigning a class to every location of an image,
  • which requires more spatial understanding than image-level classification.
  • [CLICK]
  • On ADE20K, a standard semantic segmentation benchmark,
  • the few active-vision models that support dense prediction
  • [CLICK]
  • lag dramatically behind the state-of-the-art.

  • [Timing: 0:45]
  • To better understand this gap,
  • it's helpful to consider active-vision models along three axes:
  • instantaneous vision, memory and action selection.
  • The first two axes define what we call the Observer.
  • In other words, the thing that makes sense of visual inputs.
  • The third axis defines the policy, or input selection strategy.
  • [CLICK]
  • The observer and the policy play fundamentally different roles:
  • If the observer is not good, no policy can make up for that.
  • Conversely, if the observer is sufficiently general,
  • it should be compatible with any policy.
  • [CLICK]
  • Most prior active vision works have focused on learning good policies,
  • without much focus on the observer.

Where people look depends on their goal

  • [Timing: 0:35]
  • So, we just talked about a "general-purpose observer".
  • Can we train a general-purpose policy?
  • In a landmark 1967 experimental psychology study by Yarbus,
  • [CLICK]
  • a participant was asked different questions before viewing the same image.
  • The study found that their eye movement patterns, or scanpaths,
  • were strongly dependent on the question they were asked,
  • showing that the "right" viewing policy depends on the task at hand.

Processing a glimpse with a Vision Transformer (ViT)

Vision
Transformer
  • [AI draft: not the authors' text]
  • We consider a scene, seen through glimpses.
  • [CLICK]
  • A glimpse is a crop of the scene at a given position and zoom level, which we call its viewpoint.
  • Whatever the zoom, the crop is resized to 128 by 128 pixels,
  • so zooming out trades detail for coverage.
  • [CLICK]
  • A Vision Transformer, like DINOv3, can process a glimpse
  • [CLICK]
  • into patch tokens, a high-dimensional vector per patch.
  • They describe this glimpse only.

Integrating visual information across viewpoints

  • [AI draft: not the authors' text]
  • Say we want to find the table in this room, from glimpses.
  • [CLICK]
  • We take a glimpse at each end of the table.
  • [CLICK]
  • DINOv3 with a linear readout gives the probability of table in the far glimpse,
  • [CLICK]
  • and in the near one.
  • [CLICK]
  • But neither answer says anything about the middle of the table.
  • And placing each answer in the scene required knowing where its glimpse was taken, which the model does not.
  • A person would infer that the table continues.
  • What's needed is to integrate visual information across viewpoints into an understanding of the whole scene.

The Canvas Vision Transformer architecture

  • [AI draft: not the authors' text]
  • CanViT pairs a Vision Transformer backbone, which processes each glimpse,
  • with a canvas, a scene-wide memory.
  • Each canvas patch maps onto a fixed region of the scene, regardless of the current viewpoint.
  • The two interact through Canvas Attention,
  • which lets the backbone pull information from the canvas and send updates to it, three times per glimpse.
  • [CLICK]
  • The glimpse's tokens are discarded after each glimpse, since the next glimpse may be anywhere, at any zoom.
  • The canvas persists,
  • so each glimpse is processed in the context of what was seen before.
  • [CLICK]
  • A recurrent CLS token also carries over, a global summary that we read out for classification.
  • [CLICK]
  • At every glimpse, the canvas can be decoded into dense predictions, like segmentation.

Scene-Relative Rotary Position Embeddings (SR-RoPE)

Glimpse patchesCanvas patches

  • [AI draft: not the authors' text]
  • The backbone sees a glimpse as an 8 by 8 grid of patches.
  • Scene-Relative RoPE gives each patch its position in the scene, computed from the glimpse's viewpoint.
  • [CLICK]
  • Canvas patches have fixed positions in the same scene coordinates.
  • Scene-relative positions bind the retinotopic backbone and the spatiotopic canvas with a shared reference frame.
  • [CLICK]
  • When the model zooms in, the glimpse's patches cluster in a small region of the scene.
  • Their spacing tells the model the zoom level.

Canvas Attention

  • [AI draft: not the authors' text]
  • Canvas Attention connects a small set of glimpse tokens with a much larger set of canvas tokens.
  • [CLICK]
  • It is asymmetric.
  • All the learned projections are on the glimpse side.
  • [CLICK]
  • Canvas tokens only go through layer normalization and position embeddings,
  • with no MLP and no self-attention.
  • With so little computation per canvas token, the canvas can be much larger than the glimpse.
  • [CLICK]
  • As a result, CanViT supports fine-grained canvases
  • without incurring the quadratic cost of self-attention.

  • [AI draft: not the authors' text]
  • How can we teach CanViT to see,
  • in a way that depends neither on the task nor on the viewing policy, and without labels?
  • Pretraining the observer with a policy would make it specific to that policy.
  • And if the policy is learned with reinforcement learning, its noisy gradients make pretraining unstable.
  • So we pretrain without any policy, on random glimpses.
  • After every glimpse, CanViT predicts the whole scene, including what it hasn't seen.
  • [CLICK]
  • Not in pixels, but in DINOv3's feature space.
  • DINOv3 sees the whole scene, and its features are the target.
  • We use a passive teacher's knowledge of the visual world
  • to teach an active student how to see from arbitrary glimpse sequences.
  • No labels are involved at any stage.
  • [CLICK]
  • The canvas carries over, and the next prediction is closer to the target.
  • [CLICK]
  • Each glimpse refines it further.
  • [CLICK]
  • Training sequences average four glimpses,
  • yet by glimpse 21 the prediction is far closer to the target.

Pretraining policies

  • [AI draft: not the authors' text]
  • The random glimpses come from two policies.
  • R-IID samples a random position and zoom level at every glimpse, favoring small glimpses.
  • F-IID sees the whole scene first, then samples like R-IID,
  • so the model gets a low-resolution view of each whole scene.
  • Every training scene gets a sequence of each, of random length, four glimpses on average.
  • Apart from these two, the policies we test later were never seen in pretraining.

Coarse-to-Fine (C2F) and Fine-to-Coarse (F2C)

  • [AI draft: not the authors' text]
  • To test how CanViT generalizes across policies, we also use policies it never saw in pretraining.
  • Coarse-to-Fine sees the whole scene, then its four quadrants, then sixteen tiles,
  • in random order within each level.
  • Fine-to-Coarse visits the same viewpoints in reverse order.
  • By the last glimpse both have seen the same viewpoints,
  • so comparing them isolates the effect of order.

Entropy-Guided Coarse-to-Fine (EG-C2F)

  • [AI draft: not the authors' text]
  • Entropy-Guided Coarse-to-Fine uses the model's own uncertainty.
  • Within each level, it visits the most uncertain tile first.
  • Uncertainty is read out from the canvas, here after the first glimpse.
  • [CLICK]
  • On the building, the readout says 99.7% building, a low entropy.
  • [CLICK]
  • Near the cars, it hesitates between car, building and road, a high entropy.
  • [CLICK]
  • The result is an entropy map over the whole scene,
  • [CLICK]
  • averaged over each quadrant.
  • [CLICK]
  • The next glimpse goes to the most uncertain quadrant.
  • So the canvas can guide viewpoint selection, even without reinforcement learning.

Policy

No policy can make up for a poor observer. A general-purpose observer lets you use any policy.

  • [AI draft: not the authors' text]
  • So what's actually in an active-vision model? Three things. Instantaneous vision: making sense of what you see right now. Memory: keeping a persistent, evolving understanding of the scene. And action selection: deciding where, and at what zoom, to look next.
  • The first two make up what we call the observer. The third is the policy.
  • Prior work often focused on the policy, on learning where to look.
  • No policy can make up for a poor observer. And a general-purpose observer lets you use any policy, at least any of the ones we tested.
  • Look at prior models' own ablations: on natural images, with a glance at the whole scene or several glimpses, replacing the chosen viewpoints with random ones costs GFNet 1.5 points on ImageNet, and AME 1 point on SUN360. Where what matters is small and off-centre, it's different: on traffic signs in large road photos, GFNet's learned policy goes from 69 to 91.5% over random patches. So where to look can matter a lot, but on natural scenes the observer carries most of the accuracy.

Dense latent distillation

  • [AI draft: not the authors' text]
  • We have the architecture. How do we pretrain it into a foundation model? We treat it as representation learning, not as an RL problem. Two things we want.
  • First, the model has to work with any viewing policy. Where people look depends on the goal, as Yarbus showed, and a foundation model serves many goals. Pretrain the observer on one policy's glimpses and it co-adapts with that policy, even with a stop-gradient; with another policy, there's no guarantee. And if the policy is learned with RL jointly with the observer, its gradients make training unstable.
  • Second, it has to understand the visual world. Learning that from scratch takes the kind of compute that went into DINOv3. But DINOv3 already has that knowledge, for a scene seen whole; our model has to learn to build it from glimpses. And no labels anywhere: DINOv3 learned from images alone, and our model learns from DINOv3's features.
  • Our answers: policy-agnostic pretraining, and dense latent distillation.

Before knowing where to look,
we should know how to see.

  • [AI draft: not the authors' text]
  • We have the architecture. How do we pretrain it into a foundation model? First, as a representation learning problem: learning to build a representation of the scene from glimpses. Not as an RL problem, which brings noisy, high-variance gradients, a sparse learning signal, and the observer coupled to its policy. Before knowing where to look, we should know how to see.

AdaGlimpse's learned viewing policies

  • [AI draft: not the authors' text]
  • So how do you pretrain an observer that has to handle any sequence of fixations? The usual approach is to train it together with a policy. Here's what AdaGlimpse's learned policies do, averaged over all ImageNet test images, step by step.
  • For reconstruction: the whole image, then the four quadrants, in the same order, for nearly every image. For classification: the whole image, then mostly the center.
  • Two separate issues. Co-adaptation: after pretraining on random patches, the observer is trained together with its policy, on the glimpses that policy picks, so it becomes specific to that policy. It may do great with it, but swap the policy, or replay human scanpaths, and there's no guarantee. And the policy is learned with reinforcement learning, here soft actor-critic, which is where instability comes from.
  • And why pay for all that, if the learned policy ends up this basic?

Accuracy against training compute

Not counted: training the teachers and pretrained weights each model starts from.

  • [AI draft: not the authors' text]
  • This is where we compare with prior active models, each at its best published result. CanViT-B, frozen with a linear readout: 45.9 mIoU on ADE20K, against 27.6 for AME; on ImageNet, 81.1 frozen and 84.5 fine-tuned, against 82.2 for AdaptiveNN.
  • Efficiency at inference is nice, but what matters scientifically is the cost of training. Here's each model's best accuracy against its own training compute; the teachers' and inherited weights' training isn't counted on any side.
  • CanViT-B's pretraining is 52.5 exaFLOPs plus 2.8 for the teacher's features. AdaGlimpse: 63 to 145 on ImageNet, 84 to 167 on ADE20K; AdaptiveNN 22.6; AME under 2, but it starts from SETR weights already trained on ADE20K.
  • So CanViT isn't cheaper than every prior model. On ADE20K it's far more accurate for a comparable budget. On ImageNet, AdaptiveNN uses less than half the compute: CanViT frozen is 1.1 points below it, fine-tuned 2.3 above. And the same pretraining serves both tasks.

The canvas

readwrite
  • [AI draft: not the authors' text]
  • So here's the idea. Next to the scene, keep a grid of latent vectors laid over the whole scene, in scene coordinates: the canvas. Before any glimpse, it holds a learned starting state.
  • The first glimpse comes in. It's processed, and it writes into the canvas, at the place in the scene where it was taken. Notice that it doesn't only change its own footprint: it already says something about the rest.
  • The second glimpse, somewhere else. Before writing, it reads the canvas: what the first glimpse left there shapes how this one is processed. Then it writes, in the same scene coordinates, so the two end up in the same place, a single representation of the scene.
  • And so on, glimpse after glimpse. Wherever you look, at whatever zoom, it all accumulates in the same canvas. That's the Canvas Vision Transformer.
