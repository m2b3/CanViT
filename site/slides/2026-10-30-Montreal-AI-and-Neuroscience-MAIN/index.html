Computational models of biological visual processing

Modeling vision with neural networks is an old idea. Hubel and Wiesel found receptive fields in cat visual cortex. Fukushima built the Neocognitron on that idea, layer after layer. Felleman and Van Essen mapped the hierarchy of visual areas. Then deep networks trained to recognize objects turned out to predict responses in inferotemporal cortex, and the better a network was at the task, the better it predicted the brain. Brain-Score now ranks hundreds of networks that way; look at the inset: past about 70 percent on ImageNet, the standard object-recognition benchmark, the relation flattens. Today, vision foundation models are among the models used to model brain function. And all of these models see an image the same way: the whole image, all at once. I'll call that passive vision.

Human vision is fundamentally active

Yarbus 1967, Eye Movements and Vision

That is not how we see. We have sharp central vision and blurry peripheral vision, and we move our eyes several times per second, toward what matters. Yarbus recorded it in the sixties: on a face, the eyes keep going back to the eyes and the mouth. And where we look depends on what we are trying to do: one painting, seven questions, seven scanpaths. It's sequential. There's planning; there's working memory, carrying evidence from one fixation to the next; and there's top-down feedback: what you saw before shapes how you process what you see now.

Integrating multiple viewpoints into a coherent understanding

Here's a room. Suppose I see one corner of the table, then another corner: two glimpses, two small views of the scene. The naïve approach is to paste the understanding from here and paste the understanding from there. This is a strong passive model, DINOv3, more on it in a minute, run on each glimpse alone, its answer put back where the glimpse was taken: how likely each spot is to be table, bright for yes. What about the space in between? No clue. But you know what's there: more table. A smart observer should infer that the table extends between them, because it understands objectness and has knowledge of the world. And that same knowledge is what lets you skip looking there.

Where does a machine get that kind of knowledge? From foundation models: trained once, on a huge amount of images, without labels, then used for many tasks. This is DINOv3. Here's a living room. [click] DINOv3 turns every small patch of the image into a list of 768 numbers. I can't show you 768 numbers, so here are three directions of them, as colors. The TV, the fireplace, the windows, the armchair, the floor: each stands out in its own color, and nobody ever told it what any of these are. [click] And that knowledge is easy to read out. One linear layer, a weighted sum of those 768 numbers per patch, trained on ADE20K, scene photos labeled pixel by pixel, says where the television is, where the fireplace is, where the armchair is. Bright means yes. That's how you use a foundation model: freeze it, put a linear layer on top. These models are among today's best models of visual cortex. But they're passive: one image, all at once.

A brief history of deep active computer vision

where to look, learned by reinforcement learning

Machines that look around have a history too. The deep-learning line usually starts in 2014 with the Recurrent Attention Model: one network takes a glimpse, sharp in the middle and blurry around it, and learns where to look next by trial and error, from whether its final answer was right; the computation no longer grows with the size of the image. [click] DRAM took it from toy digits to house numbers in street photographs, read digit by digit. [click] Then not much on real images until 2019: Saccader brought it to ImageNet, about 70 percent, 75 with a bigger classifier reading its glimpses, thanks to a pretraining step that made the reinforcement learning work. [click] GFNet: glance at the whole image, then focus on patches only when needed, and stop once confident; active vision as a speed-up. [click] AME segments the whole scene from its glimpses, and picks the next one where its decoder's attention is most spread out; no reinforcement learning there. [click] AdaGlimpse chooses both where to look and how much to zoom. [click] And from GFNet's group, AdaptiveNN, last year: coarse-to-fine fixations across many tasks, compared with where people look, as measured by mouse tracking. [click] Notice one thing: of these seven, all but one learn where to look by reinforcement learning. Most of these papers put their novelty into choosing where to look. [click] And this year, at NeurIPS: CanViT. It learns no policy at all; it is the observer, and you bring the policy.

The wide gap between passive and active computer vision

How do you measure how smart a vision model is? Two standard tests. The first is classification, on ImageNet: one photo, one label for the whole image, out of a thousand classes, as fine as the breed of a dog. [click] English springer. One good look at the right part, here the head, and you have it. [click] Passive models of the same size as the active ones, over the years, trained end to end. [click] And frozen self-supervised features with just a linear layer on top: that's how foundation models are read out. [click] Now the active models from the timeline. Below the models trained end to end, every time. Two of them edge above the self-supervised features of their day, which still trailed supervised training back then. [click] The second test is much harder: semantic segmentation, on ADE20K. [click] A label for every pixel: wall, floor, sofa, the book on the coffee table; 150 classes. You have to know what is everywhere, including where you haven't looked closely. For a model that sees one glimpse at a time, that means remembering what it saw where, and guessing the rest. Most active models can't produce it at all. The score is mIoU: for each class, how much the predicted region overlaps the true one, averaged over classes. [click] Passive, frozen features, and the two active models that do it. [click] That's the gap. Active computer vision was not nearly as smart as passive computer vision. Even with lots of training, lots of data and lots of inference compute, it was terrible. The whole point was supposed to be efficiency; instead, on segmentation, worse accuracy and worse efficiency at every level.

Why was it stuck? I think people were focusing on the wrong thing. I made the same mistake as everybody else: I focused on choosing where to look. An active-vision model does three things: make sense of what it sees right now, keep an evolving understanding of the scene, and decide where to look next. The first two make the observer: the thing that makes sense of the world given some inputs. The third is the policy. Prior work mostly worked on the policy, usually with reinforcement learning: a new algorithm, a new heuristic. [click] What did it buy? Two of these papers checked, in their own ablations: the same model, with its viewpoints chosen at random. GFNet on ImageNet: 74.5 at random, 75.9 with its learned policy. AME on scene classification: 72.4 against 73.4. About a point, where the gap to passive models was several points on ImageNet and over twenty on segmentation. [click] It doesn't matter what policy I have if I don't have a good observer. Once there is a good observer, the policy does matter, most when you can only take a few glimpses; we'll see that. Give a perfect observer enough glimpses of a static scene, and a naive policy ends up where a smart one does. And there was no good observer. There were good computer-vision models, but passive ones: nothing integrated what they saw into a global scene understanding. So, build the observer first. A general-purpose observer lets you use any policy.

CanViT in action

Here's how it works. A scene: a street. [click] CanViT never sees it whole. It looks at one viewpoint at a time: a position, x and y, and a scale, how zoomed in. [click] What's inside the viewpoint is cropped and resized to 128 by 128 pixels, whatever the zoom: that's the glimpse, all the model gets. Outside it there's nothing, not even a blur, and there's a world of difference between seeing something blurry and seeing nothing at all. [click] The glimpse goes into CanViT, [click] which writes what it understood into its canvas: a memory of the whole scene, a vector for every small region of it. As for DINOv3, the colors are three directions of those vectors: after one glimpse, already a guess at the whole street. [click] Then the next viewpoint, the next glimpse, and so on. It takes glimpses and gradually paints its understanding, including places it hasn't looked at yet. [click] And at any moment the canvas can be read out with a linear layer: here as segmentation, a class at every position.

Canvas Vision Transformer architecture

How does it work inside? The street again, and its first glimpse. A Vision Transformer, the architecture behind DINOv3 and most vision models today, processes the glimpse, cut into small patches, through twelve blocks. [click] Above it, the canvas: a memory for the whole scene, a grid of vectors, one per small region of the scene, each staying at its place in the scene whatever the glimpse: in your terms, the glimpse stream is retinotopic and the canvas spatiotopic. It is 32 by 32 in training, and I can make it finer, 64 by 64, at test time, without retraining the model. Before the first glimpse, it's the same everywhere. [click] Now the glimpse goes through. Every few blocks the two streams talk. A read: the glimpse stream queries the canvas, so what you saw before shapes how you process what you see now: top-down feedback. A write: the canvas queries the glimpse stream, here is what the glimpse stream holds at that point, and the canvas takes in what it finds. Read, write, three times. Watch the canvas fill: after the first write, the glimpse's own square; after the second, shapes of the things inside it; after the third, structure beyond the glimpse, across the street. I put the intelligence on the Vision Transformer side, and made the memory dumb, but not too dumb: the canvas never goes through a learned projection, only a normalization. Just dumb enough that I can read and write it cheaply, after every glimpse, at high resolution; flexible enough to fill in unseen things from what it knows of the world, like the table. [click] The next glimpse: the glimpse stream starts over, the canvas carries over, and the writes add the buildings. After every glimpse I can ask, at any point in time and for any position: what does the model think is there?

How do you train it? The other thing that made it work was separating the architecture from the problem of learning good representations. We already know what good visual representations look like. So we distill: a student learns to reproduce a teacher's outputs. The teacher is passive, DINOv3, and sees the whole scene; the student is active, CanViT, and only gets glimpses. Dense, because it predicts a feature for every patch; latent, because it predicts features, not pixels. Here is a rollout like those in training, unrolled in time; in training they are four glimpses long on average, this one runs to twenty-one. The input: a glimpse, 128 by 128 pixels, and where it was taken: x, y and the scale. Here, a piece of bare wall. From that alone, CanViT has to output a prediction for the whole scene, every patch, seen or not, in DINOv3's feature space. [click] The target: DINOv3, frozen, on the whole scene at high resolution. The prediction is scored by mean squared error against it, over every patch. [click] Next glimpse, somewhere else, at another zoom: the bottom of the window. The canvas carries over what it had; a new prediction of the whole scene, scored against the same target. [click] Again: the pillow. [click] And so on, here up to the twenty-first glimpse. In this example the error falls glimpse after glimpse, to about a third of where it started. No labels anywhere. Is it cheating to learn from a model that sees everything? Reconstructing pixels from partial views would be distillation too, from the identity teacher. We just picked a teacher whose space means something.

Policy agnosticism

One more thing about training: no policy is built in. Every glimpse is random: anywhere in the scene, at any zoom from the whole scene down to a quarter of a percent of it, with small glimpses more likely; and the number of glimpses is random too: two, four, six, sometimes many more, four on average. [click] Each scene is seen twice: once like this, random from the start, R-IID in the paper; and once starting from the whole scene, then random, F-IID. These are the actual draws of our training code. So the model never learns one way of looking: apart from these two, none of the policies I'll show you later was ever seen in training. We pretrained CanViT-B this way from scratch, on 13.2 million ImageNet-21k scenes and about a billion random glimpses, in 166 hours on a single H100 GPU.

A persistent, evolving understanding of the scene

What does the memory do? A street. First glimpse: a shop sign. In the middle, for each kind of thing, how likely the model thinks it is, at every position: the sign lights up. [click] Second glimpse: people walking. They light up, and the sign is still there. [click] Third: a bicycle. All three now: what it saw stays, glimpse after glimpse. That's integration across glimpses, what you do across saccades: trans-saccadic integration. [click] Same three glimpses, but the memory is wiped before each one: only the bicycle. Unless you get a good look at something, the representation is bad. This is a clean example; over the sequences we tried across ADE20K, an object seen once and then looked away from keeps a probability around 0.4 to 0.45 with the memory, against 0.04 when it is reset.

Spatial coverage and perception of detail

Remember: whatever the zoom, the model gets 128 by 128 pixels. That's the trade-off: spatial coverage against perception of detail. Here's a billiard room. [click] Look at the whole scene, and this is what the model receives: everything, coarsely. Is there a television? The model says no. [click] Because here is all it had of the television: a few pixels. [click] Now zoom in on that spot: same number of pixels, all on the television. And now the model sees it. [click] A passive model given the whole scene in 128 pixels is stuck where we were two clicks ago: here is DINOv3, on that same coarse view of the whole room. Nothing. It can't go and look. This example is a clean one. Over four thousand small objects in ADE20K, a zoomed glimpse almost doubles how much of the object the model gets right, from 18 to 32 percent, while showing the whole scene a second time barely helps, 19 percent: it's the zoom, not the extra step.

Viewing policies

Because CanViT was pretrained with no policy built in, we can try any policy on it. Here are the six in the paper, one at a time, on the same street, each in its color from the paper's figures. R-IID: random places and zoom levels, exactly how it was pretrained. [click] F-IID: the same, but the first glimpse is the full scene; that's the other half of pretraining. [click] C2F, coarse to fine: the whole scene, then the four quarters, then the sixteenths, in random order within each level. The natural order, and the one human scene recognition follows: shown the same filtered scenes coarse to fine or fine to coarse, people categorize them faster coarse to fine (Kauffmann and colleagues, 2015). [click] F2C: exactly C2F's viewpoints, in reverse, finest first. By the end it has seen the same thing; any difference comes from the order. [click] EG-C2F: C2F's levels, but within each level it looks first where the model is least sure about what's there. More on that in a second. [click] And RFS: the full scene, again and again. More processing, no new input; it separates thinking longer from seeing more.

Uncertainty-based viewpoint selection

EG-C2F looks first where the model is least sure. How does it know where that is? After one glimpse of the whole street, read out from the canvas what the model thinks is at each position: a probability for each of the 150 classes. [click] Here, on the building: building, almost certain. [click] Here, by the car: car, building, road. It doesn't know. [click] How flat that distribution is, we call its entropy, in bits: zero when one class takes everything; here 3.2 bits, as unsure as choosing among about nine equally likely classes; at most 7.2 bits for all 150. Over the whole scene, that's a map of the model's own uncertainty about what is where. A kind of metacognition, taking the word broadly: a readout of its own uncertainty about what is where. And looking next where you are least certain is a rule people seem to use too: in a shape-learning task, Renninger and colleagues found people's next fixations predicted by just this kind of local rule. [click] EG-C2F averages it over each quarter of the scene, [click] and looks next where it's highest. No reinforcement learning, no training: it reads what the canvas already holds. AME, in the timeline, did something similar with its attention maps.

Why should you care what a model costs to train? Because compute is money and time: a model that is cheap to train and adapt makes every experiment cheaper, and puts it within reach of a lab without a cluster. So here is what each active model cost to train, its own training only, against the best accuracy it reached. ImageNet first. CanViT-B, pretrained from scratch: 55 exaflops, that is 166 hours on one H100 plus 8 hours to compute the teacher's features. Frozen, with a linear layer: 81.1. Fine-tuned, 5 exaflops more, under 15 hours on four cloud TPU chips: 84.5. AdaGlimpse spent 63 to 145 exaflops, depending on whether you believe its code or its paper about how many glimpses it pretrained on, for 77.5. AdaptiveNN doesn't report its cost; counted from its code, about 23 exaflops for 82.2: cheaper than us, but it is a classifier, trained for this one task. [click] Segmentation. Remember where we started: networks that got good at vision turned out to be good models of the visual cortex. Active models were never good enough at vision to enter that comparison. CanViT gets close to the passive models it learned from, so that comparison can start. So what does this give us? An observer we can ask questions about active vision. [click] Top-down feedback: how what you saw changes how you process what you see next. Coarse-to-fine against fine-to-coarse: by the end, the same views, in another order, and a different result. [click] Trans-saccadic memory: what is kept from one view to the next. With the memory, the sign, the people and the bicycle; without it, only the last thing seen. What it keeps, for which objects, for how long, can now be measured and set beside what people keep across saccades. [click] Scanpaths: on the same observer, a human scanpath is just another policy, so it can be compared with coarse-to-fine or with a policy that looks where it is uncertain. It has limits: static scenes, and it needs a passive teacher. Others are moving this way: Thorat and colleagues trained models to predict the next glimpse along human scanpaths and got representations aligned with 7 tesla fMRI. We hope CanViT is the first of many Active-Vision Foundation Models.

Canvas Attention

The canvas talks to the glimpse stream through Canvas Attention. In a write, the canvas queries the glimpse; in a read, the glimpse queries the canvas. Here's the trick: it's asymmetric. Every learned projection, the queries, keys, values and outputs, sits on the glimpse side. The canvas side only gets a LayerNorm and its position. The canvas never goes through a learned projection: no MLP, no self-attention, no query, key, value or output weights; only a LayerNorm. So the memory is dumb, on purpose. But not too dumb. Just dumb enough that I could interact with it cheaply, while leaving it flexible enough to reconstruct unseen things from world-knowledge priors, like the table. That's what lets the canvas be large and fine-grained, and read after every glimpse. Previous dense models had a cheap encoder and a massive decoder. Here, a read and a write on a 64 by 64 canvas cost 2.8 gigaflops; with projections on both sides it would be 37.3.
