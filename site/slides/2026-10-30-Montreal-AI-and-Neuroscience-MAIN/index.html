Computational models of biological visual processing

Modeling vision with neural networks is an old idea. Hubel and Wiesel found receptive fields in cat visual cortex. Fukushima built a network on that idea, layer after layer. Felleman and Van Essen mapped the hierarchy of visual areas. Then deep networks trained to recognize objects turned out to predict responses in inferotemporal cortex, and the better a network was at the task, the better it predicted the brain. Brain-Score now ranks hundreds of networks that way; look at the inset: past about 70 percent on ImageNet, the relation flattens. Today, vision foundation models are among the models used to model brain function. And all of these models see an image the same way: the whole image, all at once.

Human vision is fundamentally active

Yarbus 1967, Eye Movements and Vision

That is not how we see. We have sharp central vision and blurry peripheral vision, and we move our eyes several times per second, toward what matters. Yarbus recorded it in the sixties: on a face, the eyes keep going back to the eyes and the mouth. And where we look depends on what we are trying to do: one painting, seven questions, seven scanpaths. It's sequential. There's planning; there's working memory, carrying evidence from one fixation to the next; and there's top-down feedback: what you saw before shapes how you process what you see now.

Integrating multiple viewpoints into a coherent understanding

Here's a room. Suppose I see one corner of the table, then another corner. The naïve approach is to paste the understanding from here and paste the understanding from there. This is a strong passive model, DINOv3, run on each glimpse alone, its answer put back where the glimpse was taken. What about the space in between? No clue. But you know what's there: more table. A smart observer should infer that the table extends between them, because it understands objectness and has knowledge of the world. And that same knowledge is what lets you skip looking there.

Where does a machine get that kind of knowledge? From foundation models: trained once, on a huge amount of images, without labels, then used for many tasks. This is DINOv3. Here's a living room. [click] DINOv3 turns every small patch of the image into a list of 768 numbers. I can't show you 768 numbers, so here are three directions of them, as colors. The TV, the fireplace, the windows, the armchair, the floor: each stands out in its own color, and nobody ever told it what any of these are. [click] And that knowledge is easy to read out. One linear layer, a weighted sum of those 768 numbers per patch, trained on ADE20K, says where the television is, where the fireplace is, where the armchair is. Bright means yes. That's how you use a foundation model: freeze it, put a linear layer on top. These models are among today's best models of visual cortex. But they're passive: one image, all at once.

A brief history of deep active computer vision

where to look, learned by reinforcement learning

Machines that look around have a history too. In deep learning it starts in 2014 with the Recurrent Attention Model: take a glimpse, decide where to look next, and the computation no longer grows with the size of the image. [click] DRAM took it from toy digits to house numbers in photographs. [click] Then not much on real images until 2019: Saccader reached 75 percent on ImageNet, thanks to a pretraining step that made the reinforcement learning work. [click] GFNet: glance at the whole image, then focus on patches only when needed; active vision as a speed-up. [click] AME segments the whole scene from its glimpses, and picks the next one where its decoder's attention is most spread out; no reinforcement learning there. [click] AdaGlimpse chooses both where to look and how much to zoom. [click] And AdaGlimpse's successor in spirit, AdaptiveNN, last year: coarse-to-fine fixations across many tasks, compared with where people look. [click] Notice one thing: all but one learn where to look by reinforcement learning. Most of the effort went into choosing where to look.

The wide gap between passive and active computer vision

How do you measure how smart a vision model is? Two standard tests. The first is classification, on ImageNet: one photo, one label for the whole image, out of a thousand classes, as fine as the breed of a dog. [click] English springer. One good look at the right part, here the head, and you have it. [click] Passive models of the same size as the active ones, over the years, trained end to end. [click] And frozen features with just a linear layer on top: that's how foundation models are read out. [click] Now the active models from the timeline. Below, every time. [click] The second test is much harder: semantic segmentation, on ADE20K. [click] A label for every pixel: wall, floor, sofa, the book on the coffee table; 150 classes. You have to know what is everywhere, including where you haven't looked closely. For a model that sees one glimpse at a time, that means remembering what it saw where, and guessing the rest. Most active models can't produce it at all. The score is mIoU: for each class, how much the predicted region overlaps the true one, averaged over classes. [click] Passive, frozen features, and the two active models that do it. [click] That's the gap. Active computer vision was not nearly as smart as passive computer vision. Even with lots of training, lots of data and lots of inference compute, it was terrible. The whole point was supposed to be efficiency; instead, worse accuracy and worse efficiency at every level.

Why was it stuck? I think people were focusing on the wrong thing. I made the same mistake as everybody else: I focused on choosing where to look. An active-vision model does three things: make sense of what it sees right now, keep an evolving understanding of the scene, and decide where to look next. The first two make the observer: the thing that makes sense of the world given some inputs. The third is the policy. Prior work mostly worked on the policy, usually with reinforcement learning: a new algorithm, a new heuristic. But it doesn't matter what policy I have if I don't have a good observer. Give a perfect observer enough glimpses of a static scene, and a naive policy ends up where a smart one does. And there was no good observer. There were good computer-vision models, but passive ones: nothing integrated what they saw into a global scene understanding. So, build the observer first. A general-purpose observer lets you use any policy.

Scenes, viewpoints and glimpses

Here's the setting. A scene, and a glimpse: a crop at a position and a zoom level, always resized to the same 128 by 128 pixels. Zoom out, and you see everything, coarsely. Zoom in, and you see detail, in a small region. Outside the glimpse there's nothing. Not even a blur. There's a world of difference between seeing something blurry and seeing nothing at all.

The Canvas Vision Transformer

This is CanViT. A Vision Transformer, in purple, processes each glimpse, in blue. The canvas, in red, is a memory for the whole scene: a grid of tokens tiling the scene, each staying at its place in the scene whatever the glimpse. I put the intelligence on the Vision Transformer side, and made the memory dumb, but not too dumb: the canvas never goes through a learned layer. Just dumb enough that I can read and write it cheaply, after every glimpse, at high resolution; flexible enough to fill in unseen things from what it knows of the world, like the table. The two streams talk all the time. In a read, the glimpse stream queries the canvas, so what you saw before shapes how you process what you see now: top-down feedback. In a write, the canvas queries the glimpse stream. After every glimpse I can ask, at any point in time and for any position: what does the model think is there?

How do you train it? The other thing that made it work was separating the architecture from the problem of learning good representations. We already know what good visual representations look like. Here is a training step, unrolled in time. The model gets one glimpse: a piece of bare wall. From that alone, it has to output a prediction for the whole scene, every patch, seen or not, in DINOv3's feature space. [click] The target: DINOv3, frozen, on the whole scene at high resolution. The prediction is scored by squared error against it, at every patch. [click] Next glimpse, somewhere else, at another zoom: the bottom of the window. The canvas carries over what it had; a new prediction of the whole scene, scored against the same target. [click] Again: the pillow. [click] And so on, here up to the eighth glimpse. In this example the error falls glimpse after glimpse, to less than half of where it started. No labels anywhere. Is it cheating to learn from a model that sees everything? Reconstructing pixels from partial views would be distillation too, from the identity teacher. We just picked a teacher whose space means something.

Policy agnosticism

One more thing about training: no policy is built in. Every glimpse is random: anywhere in the scene, at any zoom from the whole scene down to a quarter of a percent of it, with small glimpses more likely; and the number of glimpses is random too: two, four, six, sometimes many more, four on average. [click] Each scene is seen twice: once like this, and once starting from the whole scene, then random. These are the actual draws of our training code. So the model never learns one way of looking: C2F, F2C, the entropy-guided policy, none of them were ever seen in training. We pretrained CanViT-B this way from scratch, on 13.2 million ImageNet-21k scenes and about a billion random glimpses, in 166 hours on a single H100.

Spatial coverage and perception of detail

Remember: whatever the zoom, the model gets 128 by 128 pixels. That's the trade-off: spatial coverage against perception of detail. Here's a billiard room. [click] Look at the whole scene, and this is what the model receives: everything, coarsely. Is there a television? The model says no. [click] Because here is all it had of the television: a few pixels. [click] Now zoom in on that spot: same number of pixels, all on the television. And now the model sees it. A passive model given the whole scene in 128 pixels is stuck where we were two clicks ago; an active one can go and look. This example is a clean one. Over four thousand small objects in ADE20K, a zoomed glimpse almost doubles how much of the object the model gets right, from 18 to 32 percent, while showing the whole scene a second time barely helps, 19 percent: it's the zoom, not the extra step.

Viewing policies

Because CanViT was pretrained with no policy built in, we can try any policy on it. These are the six in the paper, each in its color from the paper's figures. R-IID and F-IID are how it was pretrained: random places and zoom levels; F-IID starts with the full scene. C2F is the natural order: the whole scene, then quarters, then sixteenths. F2C uses exactly C2F's viewpoints, in reverse, finest first: by the end it has seen the same thing, so any difference comes from the order. EG-C2F follows C2F's levels but looks first where the segmentation read from the canvas is least certain: what the canvas holds can guide where to look, without reinforcement learning. And RFS shows the full scene again and again: more processing, no new input. [click] Here they are on the same street, glimpse by glimpse. [click] And the race: each policy's accuracy over all of ADE20K validation, glimpse by glimpse (mean over runs, 64 by 64 canvas). C2F ends above F2C with the same viewpoints: what was seen changes how the next view is processed. RFS improves at first, then declines.

Uncertainty-based viewpoint selection

EG-C2F was ahead of C2F for most of the race: same views, a different order within each level. How does it choose? After one glimpse of the whole street, read out from the canvas what the model thinks is at each position: a probability for each of the 150 classes. [click] Here, on the building: building, almost certain. [click] Here, by the car: car, building, road. It doesn't know. [click] How flat that distribution is, we call its entropy, in bits: zero when one class takes everything; here 3.2 bits, as unsure as choosing among about nine equally likely classes; at most 7.2 bits for all 150. Over the whole scene, that's a map of the model's own uncertainty about what is where. A kind of metacognition: it knows what it doesn't know. [click] EG-C2F averages it over each quarter of the scene, [click] and looks next where it's highest. No reinforcement learning, no training: it reads what the canvas already holds. AME, in the timeline, did something similar with its attention maps.

So what does this give us? An observer we can use to ask questions about active vision. What is kept from one view to the next. How what was seen changes how the next view is processed. How human scanpaths compare with policies on the same observer. It has limits: static scenes, and it needs a passive teacher. Others are moving this way: Thorat and colleagues trained models to predict the next glimpse along human scanpaths and got representations aligned with 7 tesla fMRI. We hope CanViT is the first of many Active-Vision Foundation Models.

Canvas Attention

The canvas talks to the glimpse stream through Canvas Attention. In a write, the canvas queries the glimpse; in a read, the glimpse queries the canvas. Here's the trick: it's asymmetric. Every learned projection, the queries, keys, values and outputs, sits on the glimpse side. The canvas side only gets a LayerNorm and its position. The canvas never goes through a learned layer: no MLP, no self-attention, no projection. So the memory is dumb, on purpose. But not too dumb. Just dumb enough that I could interact with it cheaply, while leaving it flexible enough to reconstruct unseen things from world-knowledge priors, like the table. That's what lets the canvas be large and fine-grained, and read after every glimpse. Previous dense models had a cheap encoder and a massive decoder. Here, a read and a write on a 64 by 64 canvas cost 2.8 gigaflops; with projections on both sides it would be 37.3.
