CanViT, the Canvas Vision Transformer, is the first task- and policy-agnostic Active-Vision Foundation Model. This is joint work with Sabrina Du, Audrey Durand and Suresh Krishna, at McGill, Mila and Université Laval.

Human vision is fundamentally active

Visual: a scene seen through a fovea: sharp at the fixation, coarse elsewhere, the fixation moving about three times per second. Planned component <canvit-foveate>.

Fixations shift about three times per second: Henderson & Hollingworth 1999, Annu. Rev. Psychol.

Human vision is fundamentally active: we shift our gaze multiple times per second, orienting our high-resolution fovea toward regions of interest, and integrate information from multiple viewpoints into a coherent understanding of our surroundings' spatial and semantic structure.

Active vision is sequential

  • Strategic planningYarbus 1967 · Hoppe & Rothkopf 2019
  • Integration of evidence across time in visual working memoryBaddeley & Hitch 1974 · Melcher 2001
  • Top-down recurrent feedbackRao & Ballard 1999 · Gilbert & Li 2013 · Kar et al. 2019
Active vision is inherently sequential, and involves strategic planning, integration of evidence across time in visual working memory, and top-down recurrent feedback. Where we look depends on what we are trying to do: here is one observer looking at the same painting under seven different instructions. What we see is integrated across fixations in visual working memory. And signals from higher areas condition early visual processing, so that each fixation is processed in light of what was seen before, rather than from a blank slate.

Vision foundation models

Interactive: a scene, DINOv3 ViT-B's feature map as PCA colors, and the cosine similarity of every patch to the hovered one; a linear probe's segmentation from the same features. Needs a teacher-feature export (canvit_pytorch.viz) and a <canvit-features> component.

DINOv3: Siméoni et al. 2025, arXiv:2508.10104

A vision foundation model like DINOv3 turns an image into a feature map: one vector per image patch, so rich that a single linear readout gives segmentation, depth or object class. DINOv3 learned it without labels. Point at a patch, and the patches most similar to it light up: parts of the same object, objects of the same kind.

Vision foundation models as models of human vision

Used to model brain function

Yamins et al. 2014 · Yamins & DiCarlo 2016 · Schrimpf et al. 2018 · Zhuang et al. 2021

But passive

  • one frame at a time, all at once
  • no glimpses
  • no memory
  • no top-down recurrent feedback
Leading vision foundation models used to model brain function lack the active sensing and top-down recurrent feedback of biological vision. This limits their value as models of human vision, which fundamentally relies on eye movements. They process individual frames independently and passively, without the ability to reuse previous computation to guide further processing.

Active computer vision

Models that process visual scenes through sequential, localized glimpses.

Timeline: active perception and animate vision (Bajcsy 1988; Aloimonos et al. 1988; Ballard 1991) · Larochelle & Hinton 2010 · Recurrent Attention Model (Mnih et al. 2014) · DRAM (Ba et al. 2015) · Saccader (Elsayed et al. 2019) · GFNet (Wang et al. 2020) · AME (Pardyl et al. 2023) · AdaGlimpse (Pardyl et al. 2024) · AdaptiveNN (Wang et al. 2025). Beside it, passive vision: AlexNet (2012) · ViT (2021) · DINO (2021) · DINOv2 (2024) · DINOv3 (2025).
Active computer vision models, which process visual scenes through sequential, localized glimpses, have been proposed for decades. Deep active vision traces back to the Recurrent Attention Model of 2014, trained with reinforcement learning, and remained largely confined to simple tasks such as digit recognition until 2019, when Saccader reached 75 percent ImageNet top-1. GFNet and AdaptiveNN showed efficiency gains on real-world classification, with fixed zoom levels. AME and AdaGlimpse handle dense prediction by expanding encoder outputs with a decoder over the whole scene, which becomes intractable at high resolutions.

Active models have struggled to match passive ones

Despite their theoretical advantages, active models have struggled to match the accuracy, efficiency, flexibility and representational richness of their passive counterparts, most strikingly on dense prediction. On ADE20K semantic segmentation, the best active model reaches 27.6 percent mIoU; a frozen passive DINOv3 ViT-B with a linear probe reaches 47.2 percent, for less compute.

Three axes of an active vision model

Instantaneous vision

making sense of what it sees at a given moment

Memory

updating a persistent, evolving understanding of the scene

Observer

Action selection

deciding where and at what zoom level to look next

Policy

No policy can make up for a poor observer.

No policy can make up for a poor observer, yet prior active vision work has often focused on action selection. An active vision model can be considered along three axes: instantaneous vision, memory, and action selection. The first two define an observer; the last defines its sensory policy. Given perfect instantaneous vision and memory, naive and strategic policies should reach the same accuracy after enough glimpses of a static scene.

A task- and policy-agnostic Active-Vision Foundation Model

A vision model that understands the spatial and semantic structure of scenes across arbitrary sequences of glimpses, with representations that transfer across tasks and viewing policies.

"Learning to see through glimpses", disentangled from "learning where to look".

We disentangle learning to see through glimpses from learning where to look, which frees active-vision pretraining from the complexity of reinforcement learning. We focused on the observer: a vision model capable of understanding the spatial and semantic structure of scenes across arbitrary sequences of glimpses, with rich representations that transfer across tasks and viewing policies.

Scenes, viewpoints and glimpses

A glimpse is a fixed-resolution crop taken at a viewpoint, a position and a zoom level, so the zoom trades spatial coverage against perception of detail. Whatever the zoom, the crop is resized to 128 by 128 pixels. Outside the glimpse, the model sees nothing.

The Canvas Vision Transformer

  • A retinotopic Vision Transformer backbone processes each glimpse.
  • A spatiotopic canvas, a scene-wide latent workspace, holds working memory.
  • Canvas Attention reads from and writes to the canvas.
CanViT pairs a retinotopic Vision Transformer backbone, which processes each glimpse, with a spatiotopic latent workspace, the canvas. The canvas is a fine-grained, scene-wide spatio-semantic memory, which can function as a cognitive map of the scene. The two streams interact through Canvas Attention, alternating reads, which condition the backbone's processing on the canvas, and writes, which populate the canvas.

Scene-Relative RoPE

Animation: the canvas's fixed grid of patch centers and the glimpse's 8 × 8 patch centers in the same scene coordinates: tightly clustered for a zoomed-in glimpse, spread out for a zoomed-out one, moving with the viewpoint.
Scene-relative rotary position embeddings bind the retinotopic glimpse stream and the spatiotopic canvas in one reference frame. We compute them from the centers of glimpse patches and canvas patches in the scene's coordinates. A zoomed-in glimpse's patches are tightly clustered in a small scene region, while a zoomed-out glimpse's patches span a wider one, so their relative distances implicitly communicate the zoom.

Canvas Attention

Read: glimpse tokens query the canvas, so the backbone's processing is conditioned on what the canvas holds.

Write: canvas tokens query the glimpse tokens, and the canvas is updated.

Learned projections only on the glimpse side. Canvas tokens see no MLP and no self-attention.

One read–write pair, 64 × 64 canvas

2.8 GFLOPs

with canvas-side projections: 37.3 GFLOPs

Canvas Attention is an asymmetric cross-attention that lets a small set of glimpse tokens interact cheaply with a much larger canvas. In a read, glimpse tokens query the canvas: this is top-down recurrent feedback to the backbone's visual processing. In a write, canvas tokens query the glimpse. Query, key, value and output projections are restricted to the glimpse side; canvas tokens get only LayerNorm, RoPE and residual addition, and never go through an MLP or self-attention. With a 64 by 64 canvas, canvas-side projections would take a read-write pair from 2.8 to 37.3 GFLOPs.

Decoupling thinking and memory

Chart: ADE20K mIoU from one 128-px full-scene glimpse, by canvas resolution (8² to 64²), beside DINOv3 ViT-B given the same input (the paper's export: Figure 5A, Table 9).
We decouple thinking, at the backbone level, from memory, at the canvas level, which makes input resolution independent of output resolution. Canvas resolution is set at inference. Even from a single low-resolution view of the whole scene, a frozen CanViT-B with a finer canvas outperforms its coarser-canvas counterpart, and its input-matched DINOv3 teacher.

Passive-to-active dense latent distillation

Animation: DINOv3's feature map of the whole 512-px scene, beside CanViT's reconstruction of it from the canvas after each glimpse, in the same PCA colors. Needs bundles recording the pretrained readout (canvit_pytorch.viz).
From sequences of glimpses, CanViT learns to reconstruct the feature map that a high-resolution DINOv3 teacher computes for the whole scene, after every glimpse. The teacher is frozen, so its features are computed once and stored. This rich learning signal lets CanViT quickly inherit much of its teacher's world knowledge, while developing the ability to iteratively refine its understanding of a scene as it ingests glimpses at arbitrary locations and zoom levels.

Reconstruction targets

Pixels of the whole scene

masked autoencoders; AdaGlimpse

DINOv3 features of the whole scene

CanViT

Reconstructing pixels is distillation from the identity teacher; CanViT reconstructs in DINOv3's latent space instead. Scene reconstruction is a natural pretext task for active vision, and masked autoencoders and some active models reconstruct pixels. We want CanViT to build a semantically rich mental image, so we reconstruct DINOv3 features. Either way, the whole scene is only a training target: the model only ever sees glimpses, and no labels are used anywhere.

Policy agnosticism

  • Random viewpoints: random positions and zoom levels, favoring small glimpses
  • Dual rollouts: one from a random viewpoint (R-IID), one from the full scene (F-IID)
  • Random lengths: truncated backpropagation over chunks of 2 glimpses, stopping with probability 0.5
Every training rollout uses random viewpoints, random zoom levels and a random length, so no viewing policy is built into the observer. Each scene gets two rollouts, one starting from a random viewpoint and one from the full-scene view. Scales are sampled to favor small, localized glimpses. Training runs over chunks of two glimpses and stops at each chunk boundary with probability one half: four glimpses on average, occasionally many more, at a constant memory footprint.

CanViT-B pretrains from a random initialization in 166 hours on a single H100, on 13.2 million ImageNet-21k scenes, an order of magnitude more than previous active models, with one billion random glimpses. Precomputing the teacher's features for the whole training set took about 8 H100-equivalent hours.

Both the architecture and the pretraining signal matter. Trained with the same DINOv3 distillation on ImageNet-1k, CanViT-B leads AdaGlimpse, the most recent dense active baseline, at every glimpse, by 18 points of mIoU after four glimpses, at about a tenth of the training compute. And without dense supervision, the same architecture loses nearly all of its patch reconstruction quality.

Results on ADE20K and ImageNet-1k

ADE20K: 38.5% mIoU in a single glimpse, up to 45.9% · ImageNet-1k: 84.5% top-1 after fine-tuning

With frozen weights, CanViT-B reaches 38.5 percent ADE20K mIoU in a single low-resolution glimpse, outperforming the best active model's 27.6 percent with 20 times fewer FLOPs, and 45.9 percent with more glimpses. Its advantage holds even with the fine-to-coarse policy's worse-than-random viewing order: perception, not viewpoint selection, was the bottleneck. On ImageNet-1k, CanViT-B also sets a new active-vision state of the art, with 84.5 percent top-1 after fine-tuning.

Viewing policies at inference

Chart: ADE20K mIoU by glimpse for C2F, F2C and RFS, from the paper's export: C2F above F2C although both see the same viewpoints by the last glimpse; RFS improving, then declining.
Processing order matters: CanViT reaches higher peak accuracy with coarse-to-fine than with fine-to-coarse, although both policies see the same viewpoints by the last glimpse. This highlights the importance of contextually informed processing. Given a constant input, the repeated full scene, accuracy first improves purely from recurrent processing, then declines without new viewpoints.

Limitations and future work

  • Static scenes: real-time video and embodied active perception
  • Policy learning, for goal-directed settings such as visual search
  • Active-to-active self-distillation, without a passive teacher
  • Larger models and pretraining datasets
Like most active-vision models, CanViT was trained and evaluated on static scenes; its constant-memory recurrent design and efficiency make it a candidate for real-time video and embodied active perception. Reinforcement-learning-based policy learning is a natural extension, particularly in goal-directed settings such as visual search. Our distillation requires a passive teacher, which may limit very high resolutions and video; active-to-active self-distillation is a promising avenue. And we used a single model size with a modest compute budget.
