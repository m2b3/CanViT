Computational models of biological visual processing

Modeling vision with neural networks is an old idea. Hubel and Wiesel found receptive fields in cat visual cortex. Fukushima built a network on that idea, layer after layer. Felleman and Van Essen mapped the hierarchy of visual areas. Then deep networks trained to recognize objects turned out to predict responses in inferotemporal cortex, and the better a network was at the task, the better it predicted the brain. Brain-Score now ranks hundreds of networks that way; look at the inset: past about 70 percent on ImageNet, the relation flattens. Today, vision foundation models are among the models used to model brain function. And all of these models see an image the same way: the whole image, all at once.

Human vision is fundamentally active

Yarbus 1967, Eye Movements and Vision

That is not how we see. We have sharp central vision and blurry peripheral vision, and we move our eyes several times per second, toward what matters. Yarbus recorded it in the sixties: on a face, the eyes keep going back to the eyes and the mouth. And where we look depends on what we are trying to do: one painting, seven questions, seven scanpaths. It's sequential. There's planning; there's working memory, carrying evidence from one fixation to the next; and there's top-down feedback: what you saw before shapes how you process what you see now.

Integrating multiple viewpoints into a coherent understanding

Here's a room. Suppose I see one corner of the table, then another corner. The naïve approach is to paste the understanding from here and paste the understanding from there. This is a strong passive model, DINOv3, run on each glimpse alone, its answer put back where the glimpse was taken. What about the space in between? No clue. But you know what's there: more table. A smart observer should infer that the table extends between them, because it understands objectness and has knowledge of the world. And that same knowledge is what lets you skip looking there.

DINOv3 feature maps

Where does a machine get that kind of knowledge? From foundation models: trained once, on a huge amount of images, without labels, then used for many tasks. This is DINOv3. It turns every patch of an image into a vector. Mark a patch, and the patches with similar vectors light up: the same fruit, the same kind of fruit, across the whole stall. Nobody labeled any of this. These models are also among today's best models of visual cortex. But they're passive: one image, all at once.

The wide gap between passive and active computer vision

passive, best published passive, frozen features + a linear layer active: sequential glimpses

Machines that look around have a long history. In deep learning it starts in 2014 with the Recurrent Attention Model, on digits. Here's how it went. Passive models, year after year. Now the active ones. Active computer vision was not nearly as smart as passive computer vision. Even with lots of training, lots of data and lots of inference compute, it was terrible. The whole point was supposed to be efficiency; instead, worse peak accuracy and worse efficiency at every level. Look at segmentation, where you have to understand the whole scene: the best active model is at 27.6 percent; a frozen passive model with one linear layer on top does around 50. To be fair, the passive line uses much bigger models and more data. That's part of the point: active models never got to that scale.

Why was it stuck? I think people were focusing on the wrong thing. I made the same mistake as everybody else: I focused on choosing where to look. An active-vision model does three things: make sense of what it sees right now, keep an evolving understanding of the scene, and decide where to look next. The first two make the observer: the thing that makes sense of the world given some inputs. The third is the policy. Prior work mostly worked on the policy, usually with reinforcement learning: a new algorithm, a new heuristic. But it doesn't matter what policy I have if I don't have a good observer. Give a perfect observer enough glimpses of a static scene, and a naive policy ends up where a smart one does. And there was no good observer. There were good computer-vision models, but passive ones: nothing integrated what they saw into a global scene understanding. So, build the observer first. A general-purpose observer lets you use any policy.

Scenes, viewpoints and glimpses

Here's the setting. A scene, and a glimpse: a crop at a position and a zoom level, always resized to the same 128 by 128 pixels. Zoom out, and you see everything, coarsely. Zoom in, and you see detail, in a small region. Outside the glimpse there's nothing. Not even a blur. There's a world of difference between seeing something blurry and seeing nothing at all.

The Canvas Vision Transformer

This is CanViT. A Vision Transformer, in purple, processes each glimpse, in blue. The canvas, in red, is a memory for the whole scene: a grid of tokens tiling the scene, each staying at its place in the scene whatever the glimpse. I put the intelligence on the Vision Transformer side. The two streams talk all the time. In a read, the glimpse stream queries the canvas, so what you saw before shapes how you process what you see now: top-down feedback. In a write, the canvas queries the glimpse stream. After every glimpse I can ask, at any point in time and for any position: what does the model think is there?

Canvas Attention

The canvas talks to the glimpse stream through Canvas Attention. In a write, the canvas queries the glimpse; in a read, the glimpse queries the canvas. Here's the trick: it's asymmetric. Every learned projection, the queries, keys, values and outputs, sits on the glimpse side. The canvas side only gets a LayerNorm and its position. The canvas never goes through a learned layer: no MLP, no self-attention, no projection. So the memory is dumb, on purpose. But not too dumb. Just dumb enough that I could interact with it cheaply, while leaving it flexible enough to reconstruct unseen things from world-knowledge priors, like the table. That's what lets the canvas be large and fine-grained, and read after every glimpse. Previous dense models had a cheap encoder and a massive decoder. Here, a read and a write on a 64 by 64 canvas cost 2.8 gigaflops; with projections on both sides it would be 37.3.

How do you train it? The other thing that made it work was separating the architecture problem from the problem of learning good representations. We already know what good visual representations look like. So DINOv3 sees the whole scene, at high resolution, once: that's the target, on the right, in PCA colors. CanViT only gets glimpses, at random places and random zoom levels, and after every one it has to produce its best guess about the entire scene, not only the region it looked at. Watch: from glimpses of the floor, the guess is vague; when a glimpse catches the table, the whole room organizes. Is it cheating to learn from a model that sees everything? Reconstructing pixels from partial views would be distillation too, from the identity teacher. We just picked a teacher whose space means something. No labels anywhere, and since the glimpses are random, no policy is built in. (What's shown: CanViT-B's pretrained readout and DINOv3 ViT-B's features in one PCA basis and color scale, after random glimpses.) We pretrained CanViT-B this way from scratch, on 13.2 million ImageNet-21k scenes and a billion random glimpses, in 166 hours on a single H100: about a week on one GPU.

Resetting the memory

Same path, two conditions. In the middle, the canvas is kept from glimpse to glimpse; on the right, it's reset before every glimpse. Reset the memory, and unless you get a good look at something, the representation is bad. Keep it, and what you saw stays and gets better.

Viewing policies

Because CanViT was pretrained with no policy built in, we can try any policy on it. These are the ones in the paper. R-IID and F-IID are the training distribution: random viewpoints, with F-IID starting from the full scene. C2F is the natural order: the whole scene, then quarters, then sixteenths. F2C is the same set of viewpoints in reverse: by the end it has seen exactly what C2F saw, and it does worse, so the order matters: what was seen changes how the next view is processed. EG-C2F follows C2F's levels but looks first where the canvas is most uncertain: the canvas can choose where to look, without reinforcement learning. And RFS shows the full scene again and again: more processing with no new input helps at first, then declines. On the right, the race: each policy's accuracy over all of ADE20K validation, glimpse by glimpse, growing as the panels play (mean over runs, 64 by 64 canvas).

Benchmark results on ADE20K and ImageNet-1k

Back to the chart. CanViT-B: 84.5 percent on ImageNet after fine-tuning, and 45.9 percent mIoU on ADE20K, frozen, with a linear layer on top. On segmentation, that's 18 points above the best previous active model.

A new active-vision state of the art in accuracy and efficiency

And it's cheap. Each curve is CanViT taking more glimpses. A single low-resolution glimpse of the full scene already gives 38.5 percent, above the best previous active model, with 20 times fewer FLOPs. And even our worst policy, worse than random, going from fine detail to coarse, gets better performance at lower cost than the best prior model. Perception, not viewpoint selection, was the bottleneck.

So what does this give us? An observer we can use to ask questions about active vision. What is kept from one view to the next. How what was seen changes how the next view is processed. How human scanpaths compare with policies on the same observer. It has limits: static scenes, and it needs a passive teacher. Others are moving this way: Thorat and colleagues trained models to predict the next glimpse along human scanpaths and got representations aligned with 7 tesla fMRI. We hope CanViT is the first of many Active-Vision Foundation Models.
