  • [Main point: Breaking down the components of an active-vision model helps see why. An active vision model can be considered along three axes: making sense of what it sees at a given moment (instantaneous vision); updating a persistent, evolving understanding of the scene, which may in turn inform further processing (memory); and deciding where and at what zoom level to look next (action selection). The first two axes define an observer's ability to understand, persist, and recall visual inputs, while the last defines its input-selection strategy.   • [AI draft: not the authors' text]
  • So what's actually in an active-vision model? Three things. Instantaneous vision: making sense of what you see right now. Memory: keeping a persistent, evolving understanding of the scene. And action selection: deciding where, and at what zoom, to look next.
  • The first two make up what we call the observer. The third is the policy.
  • Prior work often focused on the policy, on learning where to look.
  • No policy can make up for a poor observer. And a general-purpose observer lets you use any policy, at least any of the ones we tested.

Where people look depends on their goal

  • [AI draft: not the authors' text]
  • And there's one more thing an active model has to deal with. This is Yarbus, 1967: the same painting, the same person looking at it, three minutes each time; the only thing that changes is the question they're asked.
  • Free looking first, and the eyes go all over the place. Ask for the ages of the people, and it's all on the faces; the clothes, and the eyes go down the bodies; how long the visitor was away, and it's back and forth between his face and the family.
  • Where you look depends on your goal. So a model that's meant to serve any task has to handle any sequence of fixations, not just the ones a particular policy would pick.

  • So let's put together what we'd want. We want a model that looks through glimpses, which passive models don't do and active models do.
  • It needs a memory, to keep what it has seen from one glimpse to the next.
  • It should give dense outputs, like segmentation, which most active models can't.
  • It should be reused across tasks, the way foundation models are, instead of being retrained for each one.
  • It should work with any viewing policy: as we just saw with Yarbus, where you look depends on the goal. Prior active models each come with their own policy.
  • And it needs rich world knowledge, as passive foundation models have and prior active models lack: that's the accuracy gap we saw.
  • That's what we call an Active-Vision Foundation Model. Nothing ticks all of these boxes yet; this is the goal.

Integrating visual information across viewpoints

  • [AI draft: not the authors' text]

The Canvas Vision Transformer architecture

  • [AI draft: not the authors' text]
  • Here's the actual architecture, from the paper. On the left, the Vision Transformer we started from, the backbone, on the first glimpse: patches in at the bottom, twelve blocks, tokens out at the top. Beside it, the canvas. Between blocks, they alternate: the glimpse reads from the canvas, R; two blocks later, the canvas reads from the glimpse, W, a write; three of each per glimpse. The backbone works in the glimpse's coordinates, retinotopic; the canvas in the scene's, spatiotopic; scene-relative positional encodings bind the two, as we'll see next.
  • Next glimpse: the glimpse tokens are thrown away, but the canvas carries over. So what the first glimpse wrote shapes how the second one is processed.
  • A single token carries over too: the recurrent CLS token, a summary of the whole scene so far, which is what we read out for classification.
  • And so on, glimpse after glimpse.

Scene-Relative Rotary Position Embeddings (SR-RoPE)

Glimpse patchesCanvas patches

  • [AI draft: not the authors' text]
  • How does the model know where things are? The backbone gets the glimpse as an 8 by 8 grid of patches, in the glimpse's own frame. We give each patch its position in the scene: where the viewpoint put it.
  • The canvas patches have positions in the same scene coordinates, on a fixed grid. Glimpse and canvas share one frame of reference, and that's what binds the retinotopic backbone to the spatiotopic canvas.
  • Zoom in: still 8 by 8 patches, now packed into a small part of the scene. The spacing of the positions tells the model the zoom.
  • These positions go into rotary position embeddings, so attention between two tokens can depend on where they are relative to each other in the scene, in the backbone and between glimpse and canvas. RoPE also lets the number of patches change, which is how the canvas resolution can be chosen at inference.

Canvas Attention

  • [AI draft: not the authors' text]
  • Here's one read and one write up close: Canvas Attention, from the paper. It's cross-attention between the glimpse tokens and the canvas tokens.
  • The key is the asymmetry. All the learned projections, query, key, value, output, are on the glimpse side.
  • The canvas side gets only a LayerNorm and the positional encoding, and its update is added back. No projections, no MLP, no self-attention on the canvas.
  • That's what makes the canvas cheap: on a 64 by 64 canvas, a read and write pair costs 2.8 GFLOPs, against 37.3 with canvas-side projections. So the canvas can be large, as fine as you need for dense outputs. Previous dense active models ran a full decoder over the whole grid at every step.
  • And the payoff, from the paper: total compute against the output grid. A plain ViT's cost explodes as you ask for finer outputs; CanViT's barely moves, even over 21 glimpses. And the saving from the asymmetry grows with the canvas.

  • We have the architecture; now, how do we pretrain it into a foundation model? We want a model that understands the visual world and works with any viewing policy. Not an RL problem: representation learning. So, policy-agnostic: no policy in pretraining, random glimpses. And dense latent distillation: after every glimpse, predict what DINOv3 sees in the whole scene.
  • And what does it learn from? A teacher: DINOv3, the foundation model from the start of the talk, frozen, seeing the whole scene at 512 pixels. The student is CanViT, which only ever gets glimpses.
  • After every glimpse, CanViT predicts from its canvas the teacher's features for the whole scene, including what it hasn't seen yet, and we score that with mean squared error against the teacher. No labels.
  • Here it is with the released model on a bedroom, with random glimpses. Training rollouts are four glimpses on average, but it keeps improving well beyond that: the error goes from 0.87 after the first glimpse to 0.31 after the 21st; predicting the average target everywhere would give 1.05.

Pretraining policies

  • [AI draft: not the authors' text]
  • Our answer: no policy at all in pretraining, and no reinforcement learning. Where does it look? Randomly: random positions and zooms, small glimpses more likely, a random number of glimpses, four on average. That's R-IID.
  • For each scene we also run a second rollout that starts with the full scene and then goes random: F-IID.
  • So there's no policy for the observer to co-adapt with. Every other policy we evaluate later, it has never seen. That's 13.2 million ImageNet-21k scenes and about a billion glimpses.

Entropy-Guided Coarse-to-Fine (EG-C2F)

  • [AI draft: not the authors' text]
  • EG-C2F is coarse to fine, but within each level it looks first where the model is least sure. After the first glimpse, the whole scene, read out the canvas: every canvas patch gets a probability for each of the 150 classes. This patch, on the building: 99.7% building. Its entropy, how spread out those probabilities are, is 0.04 bits: sure.
  • This one, near the cars: car 27%, building 27%, road 12%. Entropy 3.2 bits: unsure.
  • Now the entropy of every patch.
  • Average it per quadrant.
  • And look next where it's highest. That's the whole rule, no learning. And the uncertainty means something: over ADE20K validation, the surest tenth of cells are 99% right, the least sure 37%. And people do something like this: Renninger and colleagues showed in 2007 that eye movements reduce local uncertainty.

  • [AI draft: not the authors' text]
  • We pretrained the observer without RL. With the observer frozen, we then trained a policy for ADE20K segmentation, with RL. These results are unpublished. CanViT-B and its segmentation readout stay frozen; only the policy learns, choosing each next glimpse among 512 viewpoints, rewarded by how much a glimpse reduces the segmentation error.
  • Both start with the full scene; after that, the learned policy beats EG-C2F at every glimpse: 42.65 against 42.2 after the second, 44.97 against 44.65 after the fifth, against a heuristic that is already strong.
  • And the training cost: pretraining the observer took about a billion glimpses. Training the policy took 640 thousand, about 80 minutes on a single RTX 4090.
  • It's the same split as for LLMs: pretrain with a dense prediction objective, then use reinforcement learning for a specific skill, reasoning for them, where to look for us.

Dense latent distillation

  • [AI draft: not the authors' text]
  • We have the architecture. How do we pretrain it into a foundation model? We treat it as representation learning, not as an RL problem. Two things we want.
  • First, the model has to work with any viewing policy. Where people look depends on the goal, as Yarbus showed, and a foundation model serves many goals. Pretrain the observer on one policy's glimpses and it co-adapts with that policy, even with a stop-gradient; with another policy, there's no guarantee. And if the policy is learned with RL jointly with the observer, its gradients make training unstable.
  • Second, it has to understand the visual world. Learning that from scratch takes the kind of compute that went into DINOv3. But DINOv3 already has that knowledge, for a scene seen whole; our model has to learn to build it from glimpses. And no labels anywhere: DINOv3 learned from images alone, and our model learns from DINOv3's features.
  • Our answers: policy-agnostic pretraining, and dense latent distillation.

we should know how to see.

  • [AI draft: not the authors' text]
  • We have the architecture. How do we pretrain it into a foundation model? First, as a representation learning problem: learning to build a representation of the scene from glimpses. The canvas

readwrite
  • [AI draft: not the authors' text]
  • So here's the idea. Next to the scene, keep a grid of latent vectors laid over the whole scene, in scene coordinates: the canvas. Before any glimpse, it holds a learned starting state.
  • The first glimpse comes in. It's processed, and it writes into the canvas, at the place in the scene where it was taken. Notice that it doesn't only change its own footprint: it already says something about the rest.
  • The second glimpse, somewhere else. Before writing, it reads the canvas: what the first glimpse left there shapes how this one is processed. Then it writes, in the same scene coordinates, so the two end up in the same place, a single representation of the scene.
  • And so on, glimpse after glimpse. Wherever you look, at whatever zoom, it all accumulates in the same canvas. That's the Canvas Vision Transformer.
